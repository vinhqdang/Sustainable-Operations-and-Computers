\title{Anticipating demand, not only carbon: receding-horizon scheduling of deferrable computing workloads across data centres with calibrated carbon-intensity forecasts}

\author{Quang-Vinh Dang\corref{cor1}}

\begin{abstract}
Shifting deferrable computing jobs to cleaner hours and locations cuts data-centre emissions, but online schedulers must act before future grid conditions and jobs are known. This paper formulates multi-site, deadline-constrained scheduling of bag-of-tasks jobs, including data-transfer emissions, as a minimum-cost flow problem. This yields exact offline optima and fast online planning, and it is proved that myopic receding-horizon scheduling can be arbitrarily worse than the offline optimum even with perfect carbon forecasts. The paper then proposes CARMA (Carbon-aware Anticipatory Receding-horizon Multi-site Allocation), which adds ``ghost jobs'' for expected arrivals, learned from job history, to each hourly plan. CARMA is paired with a 1--24-hour carbon-intensity forecaster that combines direct gradient-boosted models with normalised conformal calibration and adaptive conformal inference. The methods are evaluated on three years of regional grid data for Great Britain with four sites and 52 out-of-sample weeks in 2024. The forecaster reduces mean absolute error by about 23\% relative to persistence at horizons of 1--6 hours and keeps its upper bounds calibrated despite decarbonisation drift. With migration allowed, CARMA reduces emissions by 36--68\% relative to carbon-agnostic operation, comes within 5.8--8.7\% of the clairvoyant optimum (against 17.6--21.5\% for myopic receding-horizon control), and lowers late work under heavy load. Anticipating demand is worth 10--13 percentage points of optimality gap, while perfect carbon foresight is worth 1--3 points to a myopic planner. Risk-averse conformal carbon pricing helps myopic planners but not CARMA. Without migration, reductions are smaller (9--18\% at the optimum) and forecast accuracy becomes the binding constraint.
\end{abstract}
